\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central question posed by this work is not whether multi-agent reinforcement learning
can control traffic signals---this is well established---but how much of that capability
survives when the policy is denied every privileged simulator quantity and restricted to
signals a roadside camera can actually compute. The experiments answer quantitatively: the
information-restriction cost is \RestrictionCost\% in mean travel time
(Section~\ref{sec:restriction_cost}), the proxy policy improves on the strongest
camera-deployable classical baseline by \ImproveStrongest\%, and the one capability not
fully recovered is the per-second gap responsiveness of the loop-instrumented actuated
reference, whose grid advantage is nominal and statistically indistinguishable
(Section~\ref{sec:res_quantitative})---a single-digit-percentage travel-time concession in
exchange for eliminating all in-pavement detection hardware. This isolates a result that
prior camera-state RL leaves implicit: those methods constrain the observation while still
training on a privileged reward~\cite{azfar2025_cosim,dai2022_worldmodels}, whereas here
the reward itself is camera-computable, so the reported cost reflects a genuinely
deployable controller rather than a partially privileged one.

For practitioners the implication is direct: adaptive, learned signal control need not
wait for instrumented intersections or privileged feedback channels. A single camera and a
legacy two-phase controller---the prevailing hardware in many developing
metropolises---suffice to host the policy, since every quantity required at inference is
derived from detection and tracking alone; matched to topology
(Section~\ref{sec:topology}), classical progression control should be retained on simple
arterials while learned camera-based control is allocated to the multi-axis network cores
where the classical heuristics degrade. The deployment barrier thus shifts from a
sensing-infrastructure problem to a calibration-and-integration problem. These findings
close an \emph{observation} gap, distinct from and complementary to the \emph{dynamics}
gap that dominates sim-to-real transfer in robotics and transfer-oriented
TSC~\cite{peng2018_dynrand,zhao2020_sim2real_survey,da2024_promptgat}: the graceful
$0$--$2\times$ degradation of Section~\ref{sec:robustness_noise} is evidence of tolerance
to bounded perception error under a documented-basis noise model, not a claim of
guaranteed field transfer, which requires the dynamics gap to be addressed in tandem and
is the subject of the companion study.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations and future work.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The evaluation employs a two-phase logic without protected turns, matching legacy
        hardware but limiting international generality.
  \item The 1D effective queue is a scalar proxy for 2D sublane flow, and the local
        throughput reward carries a minor theoretical risk of downstream spillback (greedy
        flushing) that the central critic only partially mitigates.
  \item With five training seeds the Holm-corrected pairwise comparisons on the grid are
        underpowered: the margins over the deployable baselines are large-effect (Cohen's
        $|d| \geq 1.13$) but not individually certified at $p < 0.05$.
  \item The corridor reversal (Section~\ref{sec:topology}) marks a genuine boundary of the
        present objective family: the vision-proxy reward contains no progression term, so
        the method targets multi-axis network cores rather than arterial coordination;
        augmenting the objective with explicit, camera-computable offset or green-wave
        incentives is a natural extension.
  \item The robustness boundary is established in simulation; bridging the physical
        dynamics gap with measured feature-recovery calibration is left to the companion
        field deployment.
\end{itemize}
