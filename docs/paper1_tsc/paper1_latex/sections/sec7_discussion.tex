\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central question posed by this work is not whether multi-agent reinforcement learning
can control traffic signals---this is well established---but how much of that capability
survives when the policy is denied every privileged simulator quantity and restricted to
signals a roadside camera can actually compute. The experiments answer this quantitatively.
The information-restriction cost, defined as the performance gap between the proxy policy and
the privileged upper bound under identical pristine features (Section~\ref{sec:restriction_cost}),
is only $[X]\%$ in mean travel time, while the proxy policy itself improves on the strongest
classical baseline by $[Y]\%$. The interpretation is direct: for the signal-timing objective,
the camera-observable feature set retains the overwhelming majority of information, and the
residual loss from discarding exact per-vehicle delays and network-wide counts is small
relative to the gains from learning itself. This isolates a result that prior camera-state RL
leaves implicit, because those methods constrain the observation while continuing to train on
a privileged reward~\cite{azfar2025_cosim,dai2022_worldmodels}; here the reward is itself
camera-computable, so the reported cost reflects a genuinely deployable controller rather than
a partially privileged one.

The mechanism behind this performance is illuminated by the ablations
(Section~\ref{sec:ablations}). The two reward components designed against starvation---the
mean-of-squared-queue penalty and the signed phase-imbalance term---contribute disproportionately to
the tail-latency metric: removing either inflates the P95 waiting time far more than the mean,
confirming that the quadratic penalty and the directional phase-imbalance signal act precisely where
a linear queue average and a one-sided pressure surrogate~\cite{varaiya2013_maxpressure,
wei2019_presslight} are blind. The advantage of centralized training over the independent-critic
variant~\cite{dewitt2020_ippo} further indicates that the observed gains are not merely the
product of a richer state, but of coordinated credit assignment across multiple intersections
under shared, non-stationary demand.

These findings should be read as closing an \emph{observation} gap, which is distinct from,
and complementary to, the \emph{dynamics} gap that dominates sim-to-real transfer in robotics
and in prior transfer-oriented TSC~\cite{peng2018_dynrand,zhao2020_sim2real_survey,
da2024_promptgat}. The graceful degradation observed across the $\{0\times,0.5\times,1\times,2\times\}$
sensing-noise envelope (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_noise}) shows that the policy does not rely
on brittle, high-precision feature recovery: performance declines smoothly rather than
collapsing as perception error grows, and the relative ordering against the heuristic baselines
is preserved throughout. We emphasize that this is evidence about the policy's tolerance to
bounded perception error under a documented-basis noise model, not a claim of guaranteed field
transfer; the latter requires the dynamics gap to be addressed in tandem and is the subject of
the companion study.

From a practitioner's standpoint, the practical implication is that adaptive, learned signal
control need not wait for instrumented intersections or privileged feedback channels. A single
camera and a legacy two-phase controller---the prevailing hardware in many developing
metropolises---are sufficient to host the policy, since every quantity required at inference
is derived from detection and tracking alone. This lowers the deployment barrier from a
sensing-infrastructure problem to a calibration-and-integration problem.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations and future work.}
Several limitations bound the immediate applicability of this study and define clear
directions for subsequent work.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \emph{Two-phase control logic.}
The action space is restricted to two non-conflicting phase groups without protected left
turns. While this matches the legacy fixed-time controllers targeted for deployment,
extending the framework to multi-phase and ring-barrier logic is necessary for broader
international applicability.

\item \emph{Simulation-only scope.}
Although the sensing-noise model is parameterized on a documented basis and swept over a
$\{0\text{--}2\times\}$ envelope (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_noise}), the results remain
simulation-based. This paper establishes the information-restriction cost and the
noise-robustness envelope of camera-only RL-TSC; closed-loop physical deployment---with its
additional sensing uncertainty, hardware integration, and dynamics gap---is deferred to an
ongoing companion field study, in which the documented-basis noise magnitudes will be
replaced by measured feature-recovery calibration.

\item \emph{Calibration depth of the traffic mix.}
The $73\%$ motorcycle share is anchored to Hanoi's reported motorcycle mode
share~\cite{ngoc2021_fivecities}; mode share (by trips) and intersection vehicle-count share
differ modestly, and the remaining fleet is a representative assumption rather than a
per-corridor measurement. Certain microscopic sub-lane parameters (e.g., exact lateral gaps)
follow standard SUMO heuristics rather than trajectory-level
calibration~\cite{cao_sano_hanoi}. The composition sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:generalization} bounds the sensitivity of this assumption but does not
eliminate it.

\item \emph{Network scale.}
Evaluation is capped at a sixteen-junction grid. Scaling to city-wide networks of hundreds
of junctions is expected to stress both the critic's credit assignment and the locality
assumption of the proxy pressure feature; partition-based~\cite{gu2024_partition}, fully
decentralized~\cite{chen2020_mplight}, and cross-city
meta-learning~\cite{jiang2024_xlight} formulations represent promising directions for
addressing this.

\item \emph{One-dimensional queue feature under two-dimensional flow.}
Although training is conducted in SUMO's sublane model---which reproduces lateral motorcycle
filtering in the \emph{dynamics}---the \texttt{effective\_queue\_norm} \emph{feature} is
computed longitudinally, as the ratio of summed halted-vehicle length to lane length. This
one-dimensional compression does not linearly track the volumetric count of laterally-packed
two-wheelers and saturates under dense filtering, so the policy observes a calibrated but
nonlinear proxy of true approach occupancy rather than a faithful vehicle count. A
two-dimensional areal occupancy feature (halted footprint over ROI area) is a natural
refinement and is a direction for future work.

\item \emph{Partial observability.}
The $26$-dimensional observation is a single $5$~s snapshot: it carries no explicit
queue-trend (temporal derivative) and no per-approach time-since-served counter, the normalized
green timer being the only temporal state. The control problem is therefore genuinely a
Dec-POMDP, and the quadratic ($L_2$) queue term serves as the principal mathematical defense
against approach starvation in place of an explicit fairness or queue-age signal. Recurrent or
frame-stacked observations are a direct extension.

\item \emph{Local reward and greedy-flush risk.}
Because the throughput term is local per-agent, an agent is rewarded for clearing its own
approaches even when the receiving downstream link is saturated. This creates a theoretical
incentive for ``greedy flushing'' and downstream spillback that the local reward cannot itself
penalize and that the centralized critic mitigates but does not eliminate. Incorporating a true
upstream--downstream network-pressure term, or gating the throughput reward by downstream
occupancy, is the most important direction for arterial- and grid-scale robustness and is left
to future work.

\item \emph{Throughput estimation bias.}
The ROI-exit count registers any vehicle identity present at the previous step and absent at the
current one. Under the sublane model, rapid lateral lane changes near the stop line---and the
rare simulator teleport---are consequently miscounted as exits, modestly inflating the
throughput signal. This bias is bounded and, importantly, is \emph{shared identically} by the
deployable ByteTrack ROI-exit counter, so it introduces no train/deploy discrepancy; it does,
however, mean the throughput term rewards a slightly noisy surrogate of true intersection
clearance.

\item \emph{Train/evaluate objective mismatch.}
By design, the agent optimizes a fully vision-computable queue-and-throughput proxy during
training and never reads true delay. The headline evaluation metrics, by contrast---mean travel
time and mean/P95 waiting time---are computed from privileged SUMO trip information
(\texttt{tripinfo}). This mismatch is deliberate and defensible, since queue length is a strong
correlate of delay, but it does mean the reported gains are achieved by a controller never given
its evaluation objective directly; correspondingly, the privileged-observation arm of
Section~\ref{sec:restriction_cost} bounds the cost of restricting the \emph{observation}, not the
cost of restricting the \emph{reward} to a delay proxy.

\end{enumerate}
